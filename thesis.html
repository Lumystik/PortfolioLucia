01 — Context & Problem

The EU AI Act demands transparency. But current labels oversimplify a complex reality.

Since 2023, major platforms — Meta, TikTok, YouTube — have been mandated to label AI-generated content. The goal: protect audiences from synthetic misinformation. The implementation: binary tags like "AI-Generated" or "Made with AI."

But creative workflows are not binary. A photographer who uses AI to remove a blemish is not doing the same thing as someone who generates an entire image from a text prompt. Collapsing this spectrum into a single label creates a design problem with real human consequences.

Content creators risk having their perceived authorship erased — not because they used AI as the author, but because a label said so.

Existing research focuses almost entirely on how audiences react to AI labels. This thesis asks a different question: what happens to the creators who are forced to label their own work?

02 — Research Question

How do AI disclosure labels shape creators' sense of authorship — and their willingness to disclose?

Two sub-questions guided the study:

RQ1

Authorship & Credit

How do creators interpret what AI disclosure labels imply about who made the image?

RQ2

Willingness to Disclose

Which factors influence a creator's decision to disclose — or not disclose — AI involvement?

03 — Methodology

A qualitative study that put creators in the feed.

Six content creators — photographers, illustrators, musicians, 3D artists — from Latin America participated in a simulated social media experience. I also conducted an expert interview with design researcher Chiara Di Lodovico on her AI image-generation workflow.

1

Stimulus Design

Four mock social media posts, each combining a specific AI label ("AI-Generated," "AI-Assisted," "Made with AI," "Co-created with AI") with a different visual context: news, digital art, interior design, and speculative art.

2

Think-Aloud Sessions

Participants verbalized their reactions in real time while viewing each labeled image. No guidance — just neutral prompts to keep the flow of thought going.

3

Follow-Up Interview

Structured questions explored how participants felt about labeling in practice: trust, identity, platform pressure, and what would make them more willing to disclose.

4

Thematic Analysis

All sessions were transcribed and coded in Taguette using Braun & Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis framework, identifying patterns across authorship, identity, and trust.

04 — Key Findings

Four themes that challenge how platforms think about disclosure.

01

Ideation is the non-negotiable core of creative identity

Creators don't define themselves by execution — they define themselves by intent. The idea, the concept, the direction: that's where authorship lives. AI can assist, but it cannot originate meaning.

02

Precise labels ("AI-Generated") override everything — even visual evidence

When a label says "AI-Generated," participants trusted it even when the image looked organic. Labels function as dominant semiotic signals that override what the eye sees.

03

Ambiguous labels ("Co-created," "AI-Assisted") create confusion, not clarity

Participants couldn't determine who deserved credit. "Co-created with AI" felt like it overstated the machine's role. "AI-Assisted" raised more questions than it answered.

04

Disclosure is both a moral act and a strategic risk

Creators want to be transparent — but not when it means platform sanctions, reduced reach, or audiences assuming "everything was AI." Disclosure is a negotiation, not a checkbox.

"Human work is like gourmet food and AI work is like fast food. Fast food exists, but gourmet food exists as well." — Study Participant #3, Illustrator
05 — Design Synthesis

Two frameworks for understanding AI involvement in creative work.

The findings pointed to a core design gap: platforms need a vocabulary for the spectrum of AI involvement — not just its presence or absence. I synthesized the research into two visual frameworks.

The Spectrum of AI Involvement

Inspired by Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens' automation continuum, this framework maps six levels of human-AI creative collaboration — from fully human-made work to fully synthetic output. It gives creators and platforms a shared language for describing what actually happened in a workflow.

Human Creative Agency Across Workflow Stages

Based on an expert interview with design researcher Chiara Di Lodovico, this curve maps how human agency fluctuates across three stages of AI-assisted image creation. Agency starts high (Direction & intent-setting), drops during Generation (the "black box"), and partially recovers during Evaluation & refinement. This demonstrates that even in AI-heavy workflows, human authorship is never fully absent — it's relational and fluctuating.

06 — Design Implications

What platforms could do differently.

This research doesn't offer prescriptive guidelines — the landscape is evolving too quickly. Instead, it points toward transferable design principles.

◎

Move from binary to spectrum-based labeling

Rather than collapsing all AI involvement into one tag, platforms could surface distinct visual clusters — separating "AI-assisted editing" from "AI-generated" at the interface level. Gestalt principles suggest this is both feasible and perceptually clearer.

◎

Design for progressive disclosure, not radical transparency

Full upfront disclosure may actually erode trust rather than build it. A first-layer label with the option to expand provenance details (as C2PA proposes) respects both audience needs and creator complexity.

◎

Make context matter

A news photograph and a speculative art piece have fundamentally different disclosure needs. Context-sensitive labeling — where the label's weight, wording, and placement adapt to content type — would reduce both friction and stigma.

◎

Protect ideation as the locus of authorship

Label design should communicate not just whether AI was used, but where in the creative process — preserving the creator's role as originator of intent.

07 — Reflection

What this thesis taught me about design.

I came into this research expecting to design a better label. I left it with a much messier conclusion: that the problem isn't really a label problem at all. It's a meaning problem.

Labels operate as semiotic signs within a social ecosystem — they don't just describe content, they reshape how audiences perceive effort, authenticity, and human worth. Designing for AI transparency means designing for how meaning is produced and received across an entire platform ecosystem, not just at the interface layer.

The hardest realization: in a context where visual realism and synthetic generation are increasingly indistinguishable, the question "did a human make this?" may be less important than "did a human mean this?" That distinction is what current labeling systems fail to capture — and what I believe the next generation of disclosure design must address.
